Flowsheet Simulator — user guide

The multicalci.com Flowsheet Simulator solves steady-state material and energy balances for whole process flowsheets in your browser. You draw the units, connect them with streams, choose a property method and press Run. This guide takes you from a first run to rigorous columns, recycle loops, design specs and CO₂ removal.

1 · Quick start

Run an example in one minute

  1. Open the simulator. An empty canvas shows the start page: New flowsheet, Open a file and the examples, grouped by industry with a search box. (Examples in the toolbar opens the same list at any time.)
  2. Choose Gas cooling & condensate knock-out, marked Good first example. The flowsheet appears with its feeds and units already specified.
  3. Press Run (or F5). When the status at the bottom reads Results available, the stream table fills in. Click any unit or stream to see its results in the panel on the right.

Build your first flowsheet: cool a wet gas and knock out the water

This ten-minute exercise covers everything you need for larger flowsheets. A hot, wet gas is cooled to 40 °C and the condensed water is separated in a flash drum.

  1. Click New flowsheet on the start page (or File ▾ → New flowsheet…). The setup asks three things:
    • Components — type nitrogen, water and carbon dioxide in the search box (or start from a typical mixture). Every new feed stream will list these three.
    • Property method — the setup recommends one from the components, with a one-line reason; here Peng–Robinson. Alternatives are listed below it, and any method can be picked. (Section 4 explains the choice.)
    • Units — °C, bar(a) and kg/h, or one of the other sets; feed composition in mole %.
    Press Create flowsheet.
  2. Drag Heater / cooler from the equipment palette onto the canvas. Then drag a Flash drum to its right. Each unit appears with its own feed and product arrows.
  3. Connect them: drag the tip of the heater's outlet arrow onto the flash drum's inlet port and release. The two units are now joined by one stream.
  4. Click the heater's feed arrow (the arrow on its inlet). The panel on the right already lists the three components: type nitrogen 75, water 20 and carbon dioxide 5 (basis mole %). Set Total flow 100 kmol/h, Temperature 150 °C and Pressure 5 bar(a). The complete badge appears when nothing is missing. (Without the setup, add components one by one with + Add component.)
  5. Click the heater. Leave Specification on Outlet temperature and enter 40 °C.
  6. Click the flash drum. Leave Flash specification on Temperature & pressure and enter 40 °C and 4.8 bar(a).
  7. Press Run. The liquid product carries about 340 kg/h, almost all of the water. The vapour leaves with the nitrogen, the CO₂ and the water that stays saturated at 40 °C. Click the heater to read its cooling duty.
Tip. Rename any unit or stream by clicking its name at the top of the right-hand panel. Turn on T·P·F tags (top left of the canvas) to print temperature, pressure and flow next to every stream on the drawing.

3 · Building a flowsheet

3.1 Place equipment

Drag units from the palette. Each new unit gets a name (E1, F1, M1 …) and feed and product arrows on its ports. Units that take several feeds show a spare + inlet port; connect a stream to it to add an inlet. These are the mixer (up to 12 inlets), and the rigorous column, three-phase separator, Gibbs reactor and kinetic reactors (up to 4 each). Select a unit or stream and press Del to remove it. The arrow keys nudge a selected block.

3.2 Connect streams

  • Red ports are required inlets that still need a stream. Blue ports are outlets or optional inlets.
  • Drag the tip of an outlet arrow onto the inlet port of the next unit. You can also drag from any free port and release on another port to connect, or on empty canvas to make a feed or product.
  • To re-route a stream, select it and drag its end handle. Every unit's panel also has a Ports & connections section with dropdowns, which is the easiest way on a phone.
  • To change a stream's path, select it and drag the small square on its middle run; ↺ in the stream's panel returns it to the automatic path.
  • To put a unit into an existing stream, drop it from the palette onto the stream: the stream now ends at the unit's inlet and a new stream carries on from its outlet.
  • To turn a unit, use ⟳ Rotate and the two Flip buttons under Ports & connections, or press R, H or V with the unit selected; its ports turn with it.
  • Streams with no source are feeds and must be specified. Streams with no destination are products.

3.2a Energy streams

An energy stream (a dashed arrow, orange for heat, purple for shaft work) passes heat or shaft work from one unit to another, as in other process simulators. Point at a unit to see its ◆ energy port, then drag from it onto another unit's ◆.

  • The unit at the arrow head takes the energy the unit at the tail gives up as its own specification. A heater, mixer, flash drum or three-phase separator takes it as its duty; a compressor on its design point (with a property method) takes it as its shaft power, and its discharge temperature and efficiency follow. Typical uses: a steam turbine driving a compressor, the heat of an aftercooler or a reactor's heat removal raising boiler feed water or steam.
  • You can drag in either direction: the arrow always points at the unit that can receive. When both can, it points the way you dragged.
  • Dropped on empty canvas, the arrow shows the unit's calculated duty or power. To give a unit a duty you type, select the arrow and set From to none, then enter the energy in kW. A unit already set to a duty gets a giving arrow straight away.
  • Fraction delivered (0–1) takes off losses: a gearbox or mechanical efficiency for shaft work, heat losses for heat.
  • Heat goes only to heat and work only to work. Turbines, pumps and exchangers cannot receive an energy stream. Each unit has one energy port.
  • When the receiving unit sits upstream of its source in the same process, the link is solved by repeated passes around the flowsheet and converges only if less energy comes back than goes in. For feed–effluent heat recovery use a heat exchanger instead.
  • The receiving unit's duty or power field shows from energy stream Q1 and its specification is locked to the duty while the arrow is connected. On a phone, use the energy row in the unit's Ports & connections section to connect, show or give energy without dragging.
  • Energy streams are listed in the Summary tab (with the heat and work passed between units), the Units sheet of the Excel workbook and the report's unit summary.

3.3 Specify feeds

Click a feed arrow to open its specification:

  • ⚡ Load a typical stream… fills in one of about 115 compositions, grouped in the list: natural gas (the five AGA 8 reference gases, lean, rich and sour gas, LNG, NGL, condensate, biogas), air and flue gases, reformer, shift, ammonia-loop and methanol syngas, urea and CO₂-removal solvents (lean MDEA, MEA, hot carbonate), water and aqueous solutions (seawater, brines, caustic, acids, aqueous ammonia, sour water, glycols, alcohol–water), refinery mixtures (fuel gas, LPG, naphtha and fuel surrogates, xylenes), refrigerants and cryogens, and common pure gases, hydrocarbons and solvents. Solutions are given in mass %, gases in mole %. A stream that needs its own method — an amine, a brine, urea — says which in the message after loading. For any other pure component use Add component, which searches the whole databank.
  • Build in Stream properties… opens the Stream properties calculator linked to this feed, starting from the feed's composition, conditions and the flowsheet's method: search any component, load a typical stream or copy another stream, press Calculate to see the phases and properties, then Use for (the feed's name) to bring the fluid back.
  • + Add component searches about 2,770 components (by name or formula, e.g. “methane” or “CO2”): 431 with ChemSep pure-component data (blue dot), 1,118 from evaluated literature data (purple dot) and 1,198 whose critical constants come from Yaws’ compilation (pink dot). Components marked “classic units only” (urea, carbamate, biuret …) work only in the reformer and urea units.
  • The composition basis can be mole %, mass %, kmol/h or kg/h. Normalize scales fractions to 100. The Σ line shows the current sum.
  • Enter temperature, pressure and total flow in any listed unit:
    • temperature in °C, K or °F;
    • pressure in bar(a), bar(g), kg/cm²(g), kg/cm²(a), kPa(a), MPa(a), psia, psig or atm;
    • flow in kg/h, t/h, kg/s, lb/h, kmol/h or Nm³/h.
    Gauge pressures are converted using 1.01325 bar atmospheric.

3.4 Specify units

Click a unit and fill its fields. Required fields carry a red asterisk. The grey hint under a field says what happens if you leave it blank (for example “blank = feed pressure”). A complete badge shows when the unit has everything it needs. Many units let you choose what you specify: a heater by outlet temperature, duty or vapour fraction; a flash drum by T & P, P & duty, P & vapour fraction or T & vapour fraction. Section 5 lists the inputs for every unit.

3.5 Run

Press Run or F5. The whole flowsheet is solved on the multicalci.com engine in one call: calculation order, recycle loops, design specs and any sensitivity study. If something is missing, the Issues tab lists it and clicking an entry selects the unit or stream.

4 · Choosing a property method

The property method decides how phase equilibrium, enthalpy and density are calculated. Set it for the whole flowsheet with Methods (or click empty canvas). With a property method, mixers, heaters, flash drums, exchangers, pumps, compressors, turbines, valves and conversion reactors become rigorous:

  • every outlet is flashed, so a stream can leave two-phase;
  • enthalpies include heats of vaporisation and mixing.

A rigorous unit can override the flowsheet method in its own panel.

The options below — valid phases, liquid volume, free water, volume translation, Henry's law, Poynting correction, liquid enthalpy, heat of mixing, missing-pair estimation, binary overrides and the data review — are under Advanced options in the property-method section; all start at the recommended setting.

🔍 Review pure-component & binary data lists exactly which constants and binary pairs will be used, and lets you override Peng–Robinson kij or NRTL pairs. Valid phases switches on a second liquid phase (vapour–liquid–liquid) for free water or partially miscible solvents. Automatic turns it on for an equation of state when water and propane-or-heavier hydrocarbons are present.

Liquid enthalpy from liquid heat capacities (activity and electrolyte methods, on by default) takes liquid water from the IAPWS-IF97 steam tables and every other liquid from its liquid heat capacity, anchored at the normal boiling point. Switched off, the liquid enthalpy is hig − ΔHvap(T) as in DWSIM’s default; that route gives liquid heat capacities 10–27 % high for methanol, ethanol and ammonia and 15 % high for water at 150 °C, which shows up in heater and exchanger duties.

Heat of mixing (activity methods, off by default) adds the excess enthalpy HE = −RT² Σ xi ∂ln γi/∂T to the liquid enthalpy. Use it with Dortmund UNIFAC, whose parameters were fitted to heats of mixing (equimolar methanol–water, benzene–cyclohexane and acetone–chloroform at 25 °C within 2–4 % of calorimetry); NRTL, UNIQUAC and Wilson parameters fitted to vapour–liquid data alone can give the wrong sign (ChemSep NRTL: +290 J/mol for methanol–water against −805 measured). Poynting correction (activity methods, on) multiplies each pure-liquid fugacity by exp[VL(P − Psat)/RT], worth 1–3 % in K at 20 bar; switch it off only to match a program that omits it. Liquid volume sets how liquid densities — and so volumetric flows and pump heads — are calculated: automatic (each component's ChemSep density correlation summed by volume, within 0.4 % for common liquids, with COSTALD near the critical point), the Rackett mixture rule (captures part of the contraction on mixing, but water comes out 7 % dense), COSTALD (best for light hydrocarbons) or the equation of state's own liquid root (5–16 % low for polar liquids). The correlation, Rackett and COSTALD densities carry the Thomson–Brobst–Hankinson (Tait) pressure correction — within 1 % of reference equations of state to 200 bar for hydrocarbons and ammonia — and a light gas dissolved below 10 mol % in a liquid (H₂, N₂, CH₄, Ar in ammonia-separator liquid) adds a partial molar volume of about half its critical volume.

Volume translation (Peng–Robinson and SRK, and an equation-of-state vapour under the activity methods; on by default) shifts the molar volume of H₂, He, N₂, O₂, Ar, CH₄ and CO by constants fitted to their reference equations of state (Péneloux et al., 1982). Plain Peng–Robinson makes hydrogen-rich gas about 4 % dense at 140 bar and the compression power of these gases 1–1.5 % low; with the shift, syngas density is within about 1 % of GERG-2008 and compressor power within 0.05 %. Enthalpy and fugacities are shifted consistently, so phase equilibria are unchanged; ammonia equilibrium at 450 °C rises from 35.4 to 36.3 mol % at 300 atm (measured 35.8) and from 64.1 to 66.6 mol % at 1000 atm (measured 69.7). Turn it off to compare with programs that use the plain cubic (DWSIM, most others). In code: volume_translation: false.

SRK binary parameters. Soave–Redlich–Kwong takes its kij from the DECHEMA SRK library distributed with ChemSep — for each pair the median of the data sets measured somewhere between 250 and 450 K — and the Peng–Robinson value where that library has none. Methane–CO₂, for example, is 0.100 with SRK and 0.0978 with Peng–Robinson. Review pure-component & binary data names the source of every pair.

Water–hydrocarbon pairs. ChemSep has no NRTL or UNIQUAC parameters for water with hydrocarbons. The engine carries pairs fitted to the IUPAC-NIST reference mutual solubilities (both liquid phases, 0 °C to 120–200 °C depending on the hydrocarbon) for benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, o-, m- and p-xylene, cyclohexane, methylcyclohexane, n-pentane to n-octane and isooctane, in the Aspen form τij = aij + bij/T + eij ln T (NRTL α = 0.2). They reproduce the reference solubilities within 5 % (NRTL) and 8 % (UNIQUAC), and put the water–ethanol–benzene layers at the ternary heteroazeotrope on the tabulated ones. Review pure-component & binary data shows them with their source and fitted temperature range; outside that range they extrapolate. NRTL overrides accept the full Aspen form a, b, e, f and α, so parameters from an Aspen Plus data set can be entered as they are.

Dew and bubble points near the critical region are found by scanning PT flashes when the Newton solution collapses onto the trivial one (a natural gas dew point at 50 bar, for example), and a bubble point at which the liquid splits in two is the heterogeneous one — the temperature at which the lighter liquid boils. With Peng–Robinson and SRK this is checked on every bubble point (nitrogen with ethane or propane near the critical pressure of nitrogen, for example); with the activity methods, set valid phases to vapour–liquid–liquid. Where the components never form a single liquid at the pressure given, the engine says so instead of returning a number.

GERG-2008 and the reference equations are multiparameter Helmholtz-energy equations — far more accurate than a cubic, and more expensive to evaluate. GERG-2008 comes from NIST's public-domain AGA 8 code; its fugacities are analytic, so dew and bubble points, flashes and phase envelopes work as with Peng–Robinson. Both take the ideal-gas heat capacity from the equation itself and keep the enthalpy basis of the other methods, so a stream can move between units on different methods. Liquid density comes from the equation (there is no liquid-volume option); viscosity and conductivity still come from the databank correlations. The validation page, section 42, compares both with CoolProp.

Refinery methods. Peng–Robinson 1978 changes only α(ω) for heavy components (ω above 0.491). PRSV2 gives every component with a databank vapour pressure (about 1,240) its own κ1–κ3, fitted by the engine to that vapour pressure up to 0.7 Tc: pure vapour pressures from the equation then agree with the databank to a median 0.02–0.05 %, where Peng–Robinson is off by 4–13 % at 0.5–0.6 Tc. Lee–Kesler–Plöcker is a corresponding-states equation with good enthalpies of hydrocarbons; its kij (a multiplier on Tc,ij, 1 = none) are fitted to GERG-2008 bubble pressures for 130 pairs of the GERG components (median deviation 1.5 %) and come from correlations for other hydrocarbon pairs; Review pure-component & binary data shows which, and the binary-parameter editor takes your own. Chao–Seader and Grayson–Streed are the classic hydrogen–hydrocarbon K-value methods (liquid fugacity correlation, regular solution, Redlich–Kwong vapour), with liquid enthalpy from Lee–Kesler. None of the last three is meant for water, alcohols or other strongly polar components — the run log says so when they are present. The validation page, section 43, compares all five with DWSIM.

Free water (Peng–Robinson and SRK, on by default) refers the water in every liquid phase to the IAPWS-IF97 steam tables, and water vapour to IF97 at its partial pressure, so a boiler, condenser or wet-gas cooler gets the steam-table latent heat (a plain cubic is about 5 % high) and the steam-table vapour pressure (up to 15 % low). Gases dissolved in liquid water — H2, N2, O2, CO, CO2, H2S, CH4, C2H6, Ar and the other noble gases — follow the IAPWS G7-04 Henry constants; a plain cubic makes them 10–100× too insoluble. The Henry correction applies to water-rich liquids only, so a hydrocarbon liquid next to the water (LPG, condensate) keeps the cubic’s own, small water content. Hydrocarbon phases and dry gases are unchanged. (Wet-gas flowsheets with a condensing hydrocarbon solved before November 2026 overstated the condensate — run them again.) Switch it off only to compare against another program’s plain cubic.

Rule of thumb. Gas or hydrocarbon systems at pressure: Peng–Robinson; natural gas, CO₂ or hydrogen blends where accuracy matters: GERG-2008; hydrogen-rich refinery streams: Grayson–Streed; one pure fluid (NH₃, CO₂, N₂, H₂, CH₄, Ar, O₂): its reference equation. Water with organics: NRTL, with a Peng–Robinson vapour above about 5 bar. Pure water and steam: IAPWS-IF97. CO₂ capture with a chemical solvent: the matching acid-gas method. NH₃ / H₂S / CO₂ in water: Sour water. Water with dissolved salts, caustic or acid: Aqueous electrolytes. With the acid-gas methods, enter the solvent as its apparent components (water, the amine or K₂CO₃, CO₂). The CO₂ amount sets the loading. H₂, N₂, CH₄, CO, Ar and O₂ dissolve physically; the CO₂-removal methods do not model H₂S or NH₃.
Salts and CO₂. In the aqueous-electrolyte method only the CO₂ component can leave the liquid. If CO₂ will boil or strip out (evaporators, strippers, flash let-down), write carbonate alkalinity as Na₂CO₃ or K₂CO₃ plus CO₂ instead of NaHCO₃ or KHCO₃ — 1 mol NaHCO₃ is ½ mol Na₂CO₃ + ½ mol CO₂ + ½ mol H₂O. A bicarbonate salt keeps its CO₂ in the liquid.

The simulator tells you which method to apply

The flowsheet panel looks at the feed components and pressures and suggests a method, with a button that applies it:

  • Water / steam only → Use Steam / water (IAPWS-IF97).
  • Hydrocarbons and light gases only with an activity method → Use Peng–Robinson.
  • Dissolved salts (NaCl, CaCl₂, NaOH …) with any other method → Use Aqueous electrolytes.
  • NH₃ or H₂S in water with an equation of state or activity method → Use Sour water.
  • An activity or acid-gas method with pressures above about 5 bar → Set vapour phase to Peng–Robinson.

Which method should I use? in the same panel lists the choices. After a run the engine checks every unit: where a vapour above about 5 bar is described as an ideal gas and its real-gas enthalpy correction (estimated with Peng–Robinson) is significant, the Run log and Issues name the units, the pressure and the size of the error.

Why it matters. “Vapour phase: Ideal gas” ignores the effect of pressure on gas enthalpy. A turbine taking 2,000 kg/h of steam from 62 bar / 410 °C to 3.5 bar / 225 °C gives −158 kW with IAPWS-IF97 and −156 kW with Peng–Robinson, but −207 kW with NRTL or UNIFAC and an ideal-gas vapour: about 30 % too much power, because real steam at 62 bar sits about 100 kJ/kg below the ideal gas.

The component databank

The property methods draw on two databanks. The ChemSep databank (431 components, blue dot in the picker) is the one most open-source simulators use. The extended databank (1,118 components, purple dot) adds compounds from evaluated literature data: critical constants from the IUPAC critical-property series, Matthews’ review, the CRC Handbook and the PSRK tables; vapour pressures from Wagner equations (McGarry; Poling, Prausnitz & O’Connell), the VDI Heat Atlas and Perry’s Handbook; ideal-gas heat capacities from the TRC tables and NIST-JANAF; liquid densities, viscosities and conductivities from Perry’s and the VDI Heat Atlas. Refrigerants (R-32, R-125, R-134a, R-245fa, R-1234yf …), siloxanes (MM, MDM, D4, D5) and the other fluids with a reference equation of state take their vapour pressure, liquid density, heat of vaporisation and ideal-gas heat capacity from that equation.

A further 1,198 compounds (pink dot, “compiled” in the Data column) have critical constants found only in Yaws’ compilation, Thermophysical Properties of Chemicals and Hydrocarbons (2014), where some are estimates. They were taken only with a measured vapour-pressure equation that reaches 1 atm within 3 K of a tabulated boiling point, so their phase equilibrium near and below the boiling point rests on measured data; treat their critical point, and results at high pressure or above the critical temperature, with more caution. That brings the databank to about 2,750 compounds for the property methods — comparable with the main pure-component databanks of commercial simulators, though far fewer than the NIST collection some of them can draw on.

Every compound was checked before it went in: the structure matches the formula; a vapour-pressure equation must reach 1 atm within 3 K of a tabulated boiling point (tabulated boiling points that disagree are decided this way, or by two sources agreeing); an Antoine equation measured over only part of the curve is extended through the boiling point and the critical point and kept only if the result stays within 3 % of it; conflicting critical constants are decided by agreement with a group-contribution estimate; every correlation must lie within physical limits at a temperature inside its range. What was not measured is estimated as for a user component — Joback heat capacity and heat of formation, Lee–Kesler vapour pressure through the measured boiling point and critical constants, Rackett density (through the measured density at 20 °C where the CRC Handbook gives one). Review pure-component & binary data shows the source of every value (hover over the Data column). A few compounds have no measured heat of formation (marked “no ΔHf”): they are fine in heat and material balances but must not be used in reactions. About 940 of the added compounds have a measured vapour-pressure equation or a reference equation of state; for the other 180 the vapour pressure is a corresponding-states estimate that is exact at the boiling point and the critical point and typically 3 % off between them (one point in ten worse than 15 %) — check those before using them with an activity-coefficient method. The validation page, section 41, lists the checks and how good each estimate is.

Components that are not in the databank

Two sections of the flowsheet panel add components to the feed picker. Both need a property method (not Classic).

  • Pseudo-components are petroleum fractions: give the normal boiling point and specific gravity (or °API), and the molar mass if you know it. Critical constants come from Twu (default), Riazi–Daubert or Kesler–Lee. A TBP assay can be cut into several pseudo-components at once.
  • User components are any other organic molecule, entered by its structure as a SMILES string. Copy it from PubChem (“SMILES” on the compound page) or from a chemical drawing program, e.g. CN1CCCC1=O for N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone. The engine works out the formula and molar mass and splits the molecule into Joback, UNIFAC and Dortmund-UNIFAC groups. From these it estimates the normal boiling point, critical constants, acentric factor, vapour pressure, liquid density, ideal-gas heat capacity, heat of formation and transport properties.

Under Measured values, enter any number you have: normal boiling point, Tc, Pc, ω, a liquid density, ΔHf or a liquid viscosity. Each one replaces its estimate and improves the ones that depend on it. A measured boiling point is the single most useful value, and one measured viscosity anchors the viscosity curve. In the component table an asterisk marks an estimated value. ⓘ shows the groups found and the source of every property. Edit or remove a component there; a component used in a feed must be removed from the feed first.

Typical accuracy from the structure alone, checked against every organic molecule of the databank (validation, section 36): boiling point about 2 % (median), critical temperature and pressure 2–3 %, liquid density 3 %, liquid thermal conductivity 8 %, surface tension 6 %. Liquid viscosity is the weakest at about 19 %. One molecule in ten is worse than four times these figures, mostly large or strongly polar molecules. Joback has no groups for a few structures (formates, sulfones, furfural-type rings): give Tb, Tc and Pc for those. A molecule must contain carbon and have no more than 60 heavy atoms. User components are saved in the flowsheet file and its share link.

Missing binary pairs

With NRTL, UNIQUAC or Wilson, a pair with neither ChemSep data nor values of your own is filled by fitting the model's parameters (a + b/T, NRTL α = 0.3) to Dortmund-UNIFAC activity coefficients, or to original UNIFAC where Dortmund has no groups for the pair. The fit covers 40 K below the lower boiling point to 10 K above the higher one. This happens automatically for user components and for databank pairs without data. For example, benzene–cyclohexane gets its 77.4 °C azeotrope instead of an ideal mixture. The run log names each filled pair, and 🔍 Review pure-component & binary data shows its parameters as “estimated (Dortmund UNIFAC)”. Supercritical gases keep Henry's law. Filled pairs are predictions, typically within 1–2 K of fitted data on bubble temperature; replace them with fitted values for design. To compare with another program, untick Estimate missing binary pairs from UNIFAC in the property-method section; such pairs are then ideal.

Transport properties

Viscosity, thermal conductivity and liquid surface tension come from the databank correlations. Where a component has none, or a user component is used, they are estimated: liquid viscosity by Joback (Letsou–Stiel near the critical point), vapour viscosity and conductivity by Chung, liquid conductivity by Sato–Riedel, and surface tension by Brock–Bird (Sastri–Rao for alcohols and acids). Mixtures use Herning–Zipperer and Wassiljewa–Mason–Saxena for gases, with Jossi–Stiel–Thodos and Stiel–Thodos for dense gas, Grunberg–Nissan without interaction for liquid viscosity (with Lucas for pressure), Li for liquid conductivity and Winterfeld–Scriven–Davis for surface tension. Winterfeld–Scriven–Davis overstates the surface tension of water with dissolved organics, which is strongly non-linear in composition; treat aqueous-mixture values as an upper bound. The stream panel lists which values in a phase are estimated. Liquid surface tension appears in the stream panel, property tables, the heat and material balance and the report.

6 · Recycles, design specs, sensitivity studies and optimisation

Recycle loops

Loops are found automatically. The solver tears the fewest streams that break every loop: they are guessed, calculated round the loop and updated until they stop changing (relative tolerance 10⁻⁵ by default) — two Wegstein steps first, then Broyden's quasi-Newton method, which converges coupled loops in far fewer passes. Torn streams are drawn dashed. If you give streams an Initial estimate and those streams break every loop, they are the ones torn, so an estimate also lets you choose the tear. If a loop is slow to converge, fill in a sensible flow, T, P and composition there.

The Convergence tab shows how each loop converged: the largest relative change of the torn streams at every pass on a log scale, with each point marked by how that pass was started (guessed values, direct substitution, Wegstein, Broyden, or a step back to substitution after a failed pass), and a table with the last change in flow, temperature and pressure and the component that changed most for every torn stream. A loop that will not converge usually shows it there — an error that stalls, oscillates or grows. Under Run options in the flowsheet panel choose Broyden (the default, which updates all torn streams together) or Wegstein only (bounded steps, one variable at a time) — worth a try when the history shows Broyden steps failing.

Within one run every repeat solve starts from what is already known: recycle loops from their last converged torn streams, rigorous columns from their last converged stage profiles, and units whose inlets have not changed are not solved again. This is what makes design specs and sensitivity studies on large flowsheets fast.

Design specs

A design spec varies one input until a result hits a target. Examples: process air until H₂/N₂ = 3 in the make-up gas, or a cooler outlet temperature until the gas has a given water content. Press Specs → + Add design spec. Choose the variable (any feed or unit input), the target (any stream or unit result) and its value. Bounds on the variable are optional but keep the search physical. Specs are solved on the server together with the recycles. The Convergence tab lists the Newton steps: the varied values, the residuals, the scaled residual (1 or less means every spec is met), the step length and the last Jacobian — a near-zero Jacobian entry means the variable hardly moves the target. Feedback calculators are listed there pass by pass as well.

Fixing an outlet and finding the inlet. The solver works from feeds to products; to go the other way, click a calculated stream and use 🎯 Make this a target in its panel. Pick the quantity to fix (mass or molar flow, temperature, pressure or the mole fraction of its main component): a design spec opens with that stream as the target and today's value filled in — type the value you need. When the flowsheet has a single feed and you fix a flow, the feed's total flow is already chosen as the variable; otherwise choose the input to vary (a feed's flow, temperature or pressure, or any unit input). Example: the feed flow that gives 6,000 kg/h of column bottoms.

Sensitivity study

In the flowsheet panel, + Set up a sensitivity study steps one input over a range and records up to eight results at each point (duties, purities, flows, temperatures …). After the run, the Sensitivity tab shows a chart and a table you can copy into Excel. With costing on, the capital, utility and total annual cost can be among the results.

Optimisation

In the flowsheet panel, + Set up an optimisation. Choose Minimise or Maximise and build the objective from one or more terms, each a coefficient times a stream or unit result or a cost total (with costing on). Add 1–6 decision variables — any feed or unit input, each with a lower and an upper bound — and, if needed, up to 8 constraints (a result ≤ or ≥ a limit, for example benzene mole fraction in the distillate ≥ 0.995).

  • One variable is searched by Brent's method (golden section with parabolic steps); two or more by a Nelder–Mead simplex kept inside the bounds. Every point is a whole-flowsheet solve with the recycles converged and the design specs met.
  • Limits are handled by an exact penalty: a point that breaks a limit always ranks behind one that meets it, so the answer is feasible whenever a feasible point was found.
  • The search stops when the simplex (or bracket) is smaller than the tolerance — 0.5 % of each variable's range by default — or after the maximum number of evaluations (60 by default, up to 300). A long search continues over further server calls automatically.
  • After the run every result on the page is at the optimum. The panel shows the start and optimum of each variable and the limits; Copy optimum to inputs writes the optimum into the feeds and units and switches the optimisation off. The Convergence tab plots and tabulates every evaluation.
  • A variable cannot also be varied by an active design spec or set by a calculator. The method finds a local optimum: start from a sensible point, and if a variable ends on a bound, widen the bound if that is physically allowed.

The Costs tab shows, for every item, the size, material, pressure, material and pressure factors, the bare-module factor, purchased and bare-module cost and the utility cost, and the totals: purchased equipment, bare-module cost, total module cost (1.18 × bare module, for contingency and fee), grassroots capital (+ 0.5 × the base-case bare-module cost, for auxiliary facilities), capital annualised with the capital recovery factor over the plant life, utilities, and the total annual cost. The default prices and the cost index are examples — set your own. Accuracy is that of a study estimate, about −20 % / +30 %. Mixers, splitters, valves and turbines are not costed.

Pinch analysis

The Pinch tab collects every heating and cooling duty of the last run — heaters and coolers, column condensers and reboilers, intercoolers, flash and reactor duties (served by utilities) and both sides of each process exchanger — and calculates, for the ΔTmin you set:

  • the minimum hot and cold utility by the problem-table algorithm, against the utility now used;
  • the pinch temperatures (hot and cold side), or that there is none (a threshold problem);
  • the composite curves and the grand composite curve;
  • the utility heaters below the pinch and coolers above it — the heat flows across the pinch that make the utilities exceed the targets.

With a property method, heater and exchanger curves are flashed along their path (Property-method curves), so boiling and condensation appear as plateaus. Condenser, reboiler and reactor duties are taken at their temperature (spread over 1 K). Untick any duty that cannot take part in heat recovery.

Cases

The Cases tab keeps up to ten cases — the inputs and main results of a run. Press + Save this run as a case, change the inputs, run, save again, and compare:

  • Summary: convergence, heating, cooling and power totals, design-spec values, the optimum, cost totals and the product streams;
  • Unit duties, One stream (T, P, flows, vapour fraction, mole fractions) and Input differences (every input that differs from the base case).

Each value shows its difference from the base case underneath; tick which cases to compare and pick the base. Restore loads a case's inputs into the flowsheet (undo brings the previous inputs back). Cases are saved with the flowsheet file and in this browser, but not in share links; the report can include the summary comparison.

11 · Troubleshooting

Run does nothing, or the status lamp stays grey.
Something is not specified. Open the Issues tab and click each entry. Look for red ports (an inlet with no stream) and units without the complete badge.
The run stops with an error.
Read the status line and the Run log; they name the unit and the reason. Common causes are a specification that cannot be met, such as a cooler outlet hotter than its inlet or a flash vapour fraction above 1, and a component the chosen method does not support.
A recycle loop does not converge.
Give the torn (dashed) stream an initial estimate close to the expected answer. Check that the loop has a way out for every component; a purge is often needed for inerts. When the run log says a component builds up in or drains from the loop on every pass, the loop has no steady state with those inputs — a closed solvent loop with a fixed make-up water flow is the usual case; let a design spec set the make-up flow, or add a purge. Make sure no unit inside the loop has an impossible spec. Very high recycle ratios converge slowly.
The rigorous column does not converge.
Start with easy specs (reflux ratio and distillate rate), get a solution, then switch to purity or recovery specs. A distillate rate cannot exceed what the feed can supply. Check the top pressure, the feed stage and that there are enough stages for the separation. For an absorber, make sure the solvent can take up the solute at the column pressure.
The heat exchanger reports a temperature cross.
The requested outlet temperature is not reachable with the other stream. Specify the other side's outlet T, the duty or a minimum approach instead, or check the flows.
Turbine or compressor power changes a lot when I change the property method.
Usually the vapour is being treated as an ideal gas at high pressure (an activity method with Vapour phase: Ideal gas). The run log says so and names the units. For water and steam use Steam / water (IAPWS-IF97); for hydrocarbons and gases use Peng–Robinson or SRK; with NRTL, UNIQUAC or UNIFAC set the vapour phase to Peng–Robinson. See Choosing a property method.
“Pacing for the engine's rate limit”.
The free engine allows about 60 runs per minute from one user, and many quick runs in a row trigger a short pause. Nothing is lost; the run continues after the pause.
“Flowsheet exceeded the server time budget”.
One server call works for up to about 25 seconds. A larger flowsheet does not stop there: the call hands back where it got to (torn streams, column profiles, design-spec point, finished sensitivity points) and the page continues automatically with further calls — the run banner shows continuing on the server (call 2 of up to 60) and the time so far, and Stop (or Esc) ends the run. This message appears only when even sixty calls are not enough. Then reduce stages or sensitivity points, check that every recycle loop can converge (see above), or split the flowsheet into parts by turning internal streams into specified feeds.
The optimisation ends “infeasible” or on a bound.
Infeasible means no point it tried met every limit: widen the bounds, start from a point that meets the limits, or relax a limit. A variable on a bound means the optimum lies outside the range — widen it if that is physically allowed. If points fail to solve (listed as failed in the Convergence tab), narrow the bounds to where the flowsheet converges.
A unit says “not costed”.
Mixers, splitters, valves and turbines carry no cost here. An equilibrium reactor needs its volume: select it and enter the volume under Costing, with equipment Reactor vessel.
I expected two liquid phases (or got one I did not expect).
Set Valid phases in the flowsheet panel. Use vapour–liquid–liquid for free water with hydrocarbons, and vapour–liquid where a second liquid is impossible.
My results differ from another simulator.
Compare the same property method with the same binary parameters. Use Review pure-component & binary data to see what is used. The validation page shows where this engine agrees with DWSIM and other references; its section 40 runs eighteen cases through DWSIM 10 side by side, with an independent reference wherever the two differ.

12 · Accuracy and limits

The simulator is meant for preliminary engineering, studies, debottleneck checks and teaching. Its methods are checked against independent references on the validation page, but results are only as good as the property parameters and specifications behind them. Know these limits:

  • Columns are equilibrium-stage models. Tray efficiencies stand in for mass-transfer rates; there is no rate-based or packing hydraulics model.
  • CO₂ removal methods cover CO₂ only, not H₂S or NH₃. The piperazine part of activated MDEA is fitted to measured data, and the rich end of a high-pressure absorber is an extrapolation that the stream warnings flag.
  • Sour water holds NH₃, H₂S, CO₂, NaOH and light gases only (no phenols, cyanide or organic acids). Partial pressures are within about 12–24 % of measured data on average; above 10 mol solute per kg water and above 200 °C results are extrapolated and flagged.
  • Aqueous electrolytes follow the PHREEQC pitzer.dat database: reliable to about 100 °C, usable to 200 °C, ionic strength up to about 6–9 mol/kg. Solids are not precipitated — a supersaturated stream stays in solution and is warned. NH₃ and H₂S need the sour-water method.
  • Urea units are tuned empirical correlations and warn outside their validity range.
  • Steam / water method: pure water only.
  • Costing is a study estimate (about −20 % / +30 %) from published correlations and example prices; mechanical design, piping, instruments and buildings are covered only by the module and grassroots factors. Turbines, expanders and valves are not costed.
  • Optimisation finds a local optimum within the bounds; it is not a global search.
  • User components and filled binary pairs are group-contribution estimates — screening quality. Enter measured constants where you have them and fitted binary parameters for design.
  • Size: up to 400 units, 1,000 streams, 200 feed streams and 60 components per stream in one flowsheet — enough for a whole ammonia–urea complex. Very large or recycle-heavy flowsheets continue over further server calls automatically.
  • Not covered: detailed mechanical design, dynamics and safety studies.
Before design use, check key results against plant data, vendor information or a second calculation.
